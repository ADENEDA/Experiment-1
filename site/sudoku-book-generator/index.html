How the uniqueness check works

A proper sudoku has exactly one answer. If it has two, a solver can do everything right and still disagree with your answer key, and the last few squares come down to a coin toss. Every puzzle here is made in three steps:

  1. Fill a complete grid at random, following the rules: every row, column and box holds each digit once.
  2. Remove digits, usually two at a time from opposite sides of the centre, which gives the classic symmetric look. After each removal a solver counts the solutions, stopping as soon as it finds a second one. If there is a second solution, the digits go back.
  3. Stop when the puzzle reaches its target grade or no digit can come out without allowing a second answer.

Before a puzzle reaches the page it is checked once more: the given digits agree with the solution, and the solution count is exactly one. That is what the Exactly one solution badge under the preview means.

How puzzles are graded

Counting the given digits is a poor guide to difficulty: two puzzles with 26 givens can be worlds apart. Gridbook solves each puzzle the way a person does, always using the simplest technique that makes progress, and grades it by the hardest technique it needed. The status line names that step.

6×6 and 4×4 for children and beginners

A 6×6 grid uses the digits 1 to 6 in boxes of 2 rows by 3 columns and runs from Easy to Expert. A 4×4 grid uses 1 to 4 in 2×2 boxes. It never needs more than the two single techniques (a check of 50,000 minimal 4×4 puzzles, ones where no digit can be removed, found none that did), so Medium is its top grade. Both make good first puzzles, and 4×4 works well for young children.
