How nonograms work

A nonogram (also called a griddler, hanjie or paint-by-number logic puzzle) is a grid of empty squares with numbers beside each row and above each column. The numbers are the lengths of the runs of shaded squares in that line, in order. 3 1 means three shaded squares in a row, at least one blank square, then one more shaded square. A line marked 0 stays blank. Shade every square the clues call for and a picture appears.

You never need to see the whole picture to make progress. Take one line at a time and ask which squares are shaded wherever its runs go. In a row of 10 squares with the clue 7, the run can start on square 1 at the earliest and square 4 at the latest, but squares 4 to 7 are covered either way, so shade them. Squares that can never be part of a run get a small dot. Every square you settle is a new clue for the line that crosses it.

Why no-guess matters

A picture is not automatically a good puzzle. Some pictures have more than one answer: shade two squares on one diagonal of a 2×2 grid and every row and column gets the clue 1, which the other diagonal fits just as well. Others have one answer but can only be finished by guessing and rubbing out, which is no fun with a pencil.

Gridbook runs a line solver on every puzzle. It applies the one-line-at-a-time reasoning above, over and over, as a careful solver would. If that settles every square, the puzzle has exactly one solution and never needs a guess. When the solver gets stuck, Gridbook changes a few squares near the edges of the shape (up to 30, or 4% of a larger grid) and checks again. If that is not enough, it adds givens: a few squares printed already shaded in gray, or dotted as blank. The badge under the preview tells you which happened: Logic-solvable, with the number of squares adjusted, or Needs 3 givens. The 195 library pictures are already solvable at every size they are drawn for.

Pictures that work best

  • Bold, simple shapes: silhouettes, icons, clip art and line drawings. Photos of people or busy scenes rarely survive the trip to 15×15 squares.
  • One subject on a plain background, filling most of the frame. Crop before you choose the image.
  • A grid to match the detail: 10×10 or 15×15 for a heart or a house, 20×20 to 30×30 for animals with legs, ears and tails.
  • The threshold slider sets how dark a square must be to count as shaded; Invert handles light shapes on dark backgrounds.
  • Your rights: use only images you made or have permission to use. The image is read in your browser and never leaves your device.

Seasonal ideas

Picture puzzles for the season

The reveal can match the date, which makes picture puzzles a natural fit for seasonal books and classroom handouts. If you are publishing on KDP, leave a few weeks before the date for KDP’s review, a printed proof copy and your own checks.

January brain games

For a New Year brain-games book, start small and finish big: a run of 10×10 warm-ups, then 15×15 and 20×20 pictures. Wintry subjects such as snowflakes, mountains and evergreen trees suit the month.

Valentine’s Day

Hearts, flowers, cakes, gifts and envelopes for love notes. A 15×15 heart makes a quick classroom handout, and the picture itself is the message.

Easter

Rabbits, tulips, butterflies, cracked eggs and birds. Pictures with clear outlines read best; 15×15 keeps them recognizable for children and adults alike.
